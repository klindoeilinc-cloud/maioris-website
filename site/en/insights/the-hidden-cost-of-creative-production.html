The hidden cost of creative production without a system.

Rework, late approvals, incomplete briefs: the most expensive part of creative work is rarely the creative part. What to measure, and where to start.

Marko Leo19 November 20265 min read

Ask a creative team where its time goes, and the answer is rarely "creating". It goes into rewriting a brief that was missing information, into a third round of changes because the right person had not seen the first one, into urgent requests that push back everything else. None of this appears on an invoice. All of it is paid for.

In his previous roles, Marko Leo saw the same pattern again and again: lead times rarely depend on the talent of the team. They depend on the order in which work arrives, the quality of the information that comes with it, and the moment decisions are taken. Reorganising production around priorities changes more than hiring.

The most expensive part of creative work is rarely the creative part.

Define what a defect is

Six Sigma is not reserved for factories. Its first lesson is useful to any studio: you cannot improve what you have not defined. For a creative team, a defect is a deliverable rejected because it does not match the approved brief, not a change of mind, which should be tracked separately.

Four indicators to start

  • First-pass approval rate: the share of deliverables approved without rework.
  • Average number of rework cycles per deliverable.
  • Lead time from brief to approval.
  • Share of rework caused by an incomplete brief.

A month of measurement is usually enough to see the dominant cause. Then comes the only rule that matters: fix one cause at a time, then lock the improvement into the system, a checklist, a template, an approval gate. Not a memo that will be forgotten.

This is the work of a Creative Operating System. It does not make teams produce more. It makes what they produce arrive faster, closer to the brief, with less waste. And it frees time for the only thing no system can do in their place: having ideas.
